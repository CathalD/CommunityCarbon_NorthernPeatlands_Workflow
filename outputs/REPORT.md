## What this workflow concluded

Eight cores, in two clusters 24 km apart at their extremes, cannot support a covariate-driven carbon map of this landscape. They can support two things: a careful accounting of what the cores themselves measure, and a stratified estimate with its uncertainty stated plainly. Both are produced. A prediction map is attempted, tested, and released only if it passes.

Four findings emerged from quality control that change how the data must be read, and all four would have been invisible had the supplied columns been taken at face value.

### 1. Longitudes were in the wrong hemisphere

Every one of the 22 rows carried a positive longitude (`+87.63` to `+87.87`). Fort Severn is at `-87.63`. As supplied, the cores plot in western Siberia, 7,540 km from where they belong. Every covariate extraction and every reference-layer lookup would have sampled the wrong continent, and nothing downstream would have complained, because the values are all perfectly valid numbers for the wrong place.

`01_ingest_qc.R` treats this as a hard gate: it stops the run unless the repair is explicitly authorised in `config.R`, and when authorised it repairs the sign loudly and flags all 22 affected rows `LON_SIGN_REPAIRED`. After repair the cores lie 2.7-19.4 km from Fort Severn, which is the independent confirmation that the repair was the right one.

A previously generated file in `data/raw/` had already negated these longitudes silently, recorded no note that it had done so, and labelled the result `lat_lon_consistent = TRUE`. That is the failure mode this gate exists to prevent.

### 2. The carbon density column is mislabelled by one dimension

The column `Organic Carbon Density (g/cm^2)` reconciles **exactly** (relative tolerance 1e-06, all 22 rows) with:

```
bulk density [g/cm3]  x  SOC [%] / 100
```

That quantity is carbon mass per unit **volume** of soil: g/cm**3**. The header says g/cm**2**, which is mass per unit **area**. They differ by one length dimension, and the difference only becomes visible when the column is integrated down a core.

The prior pipeline summed this column down each core. Summing concentrations is dimensionally meaningless. The table below shows the size of the resulting error:

| FS-02 |      30 |  0.0838 |  0.8382 |      10 |

Where every segment is 10 cm thick the error is a clean factor of 10 and could in principle be undone. Where segment thickness varies -- all 3 PM peat cores -- the error differs from core to core (7.7x, 13.4x, 14.5x) and **cannot** be undone by rescaling. This workflow integrates density x thickness properly throughout.

### 3. The two campaigns used different organic-matter-to-carbon factors

| group | n | factor_est | implied_divisor | factor_name |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FS_2025_mineral |      15 |   0.554 |  1.8052 | 1/1.8 (0.556) |
| PM_2024_peat |       7 |  0.5801 |  1.7237 | van Bemmelen (0.58 = 1/1.724) |

`SOC` is not one measured quantity in this file. It is two differently-derived quantities sharing a column. The peat cores carry the van Bemmelen factor of 0.58, which is the classic convention and is known to **overstate** carbon in true peat: measured C:OM for northern peat sits nearer 0.51. So the campaign where the factor matters most is the one using the least appropriate factor.

Re-converting the peat cores at 0.51 lowers their stocks by 12.1% and leaves the mineral cores untouched. Both scenarios are carried through the whole workflow rather than one being chosen silently. The supplied values remain the primary product; the alternative is reported as sensitivity.

This also means the peat-versus-mineral contrast in this dataset is **partly a laboratory-convention artefact**. Campaign, landscape type, year, operator and conversion factor are perfectly confounded. No analysis can separate them, and this report does not pretend otherwise.

### 4. `Depth` is segment thickness, not cumulative depth

Read as a cumulative bottom depth, the column decreases within cores PM-02-A (15.2 -> 5.5) and PM-03-A (8.75 -> 7.85, 7.85 -> 5.5). A cumulative depth cannot decrease. The workflow derives this verdict from the data in `infer_depth_semantics()` rather than assuming it, and would stop if the evidence contradicted the configuration.

Accumulating thickness gives core lengths of 14.5 to 30.7 cm, which matches the stated field record and corroborates the reading.

Two windows are reported and they are **not interchangeable**:

- **Common support, 0-14.5 cm.** The deepest window every core fully covers, derived from the data and limited by PM-01-A. No extrapolation anywhere; all eight cores are directly comparable. This is the window in which the sample-based products are honest, and the window the model in 06 uses as its response.
- **Reference, 0-30 cm.** Chosen to match the published layers. Six cores reach it. PM-01-A (14.5 cm) and PM-02-A (20.7 cm) do not, so their stocks are **lower bounds**, flagged `is_lower_bound`, and never rescaled to look complete. PM-03-A at 30.7 cm is truncated to 30 cm.

An extrapolated variant is available but off the critical path. For PM-01-A, 52% of the extrapolated stock would be invented by carrying a single segment's density down 15.5 cm it never sampled. It is reported as a scenario, never as a value.

### Why the peat cores hold less carbon than the mineral cores

Over the common-support window the peat cores average 3.08 kg C/m2 against 5.26 kg C/m2 for the mineral cores. This is not an error, and it is the single most important thing in this report for anyone planning to use a 0-30 cm carbon product in this landscape.

Peat here has bulk density of 0.05-0.27 g/cm3 against 0.75-2.18 for the mineral soils. A high carbon *percentage* multiplied by a very low *density* yields little carbon *mass* in a fixed volume. The peatland advantage is real but it lives in **depth**: Hudson Bay Lowlands peat is metres thick, and nearly all of its carbon sits below the 30 cm window that every published 0-30 cm product reports.

**A 0-30 cm map of this landscape is not a map of its carbon. It is a map of the top of its carbon**, and for peatlands specifically it is a map of the least important part.

### Product 2 -- stratum-mean assignment map

| stratum | n_cores | mean_stock_kgm2 | se_kgm2 | worldcover_classes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PM_2024_peat |       3 |   3.083 |   0.216 | 90 (herbaceous wetland), 30 (grassland) where wet |
| FS_2025_mineral |       5 |   5.257 |   1.057 | 10 (tree cover), 20 (shrubland) |

- **PM_2024_peat**: WorldCover does not distinguish peat depth or peatland type; a herbaceous wetland class covers both shallow fen and deep bog, which differ greatly in carbon.
- **FS_2025_mineral**: Tree cover in the Hudson Bay Lowlands includes treed bog on peat as well as upland forest on mineral soil. This class is NOT a clean mineral-soil mask.

This is a design-based mean projected onto a categorical layer. No distance-weighting of samples occurs, so it is not interpolation; no covariate relationship is fitted, so it is not prediction. Its assumption -- that a stratum is internally homogeneous -- is visibly false: the mineral cores span more than threefold within their stratum. It is defensible as a first approximation and as nothing more.

### Product 3 -- covariate prediction

Not yet evaluated: requires the Earth Engine covariate extraction from `03_gee_covariates.R`. The script is written and tested against a synthetic fixture; it needs an authenticated Earth Engine session to run on real covariates.

The model is deliberately an ordinary least-squares fit on a small number of covariates, not a random forest. ## Validation

### Cross-validation strategy

**Leave-one-core-out, never leave-one-segment-out.** This is the single most consequential methodological choice in the workflow.

There are 22 segments, which looks like a workable sample size. It is not. Every segment of a core sits at one coordinate pair, so at 30 m resolution every segment of a core samples the *same pixel* of every covariate. Segments within a core also share a campaign, an operator and a laboratory batch. A model trained on segments 1 and 2 of a core and tested on segment 3 is being tested on data it has already seen. The effective sample size for any covariate model is **8**, not 22.

Leave-one-stratum-out is also run, and it asks the harder question: train on the mineral cores, predict the peat cores. Mapping means predicting ground unlike the training points, so this is the relevant test, and it is expected to fail.

Every model is scored against a mean-only null. A covariate model that cannot beat the mean has no business being painted across a landscape, and `06` writes no raster when it does not.

Eight cores give 28 pairs in total. **Zero** of the five lag bins reach the ~30 pairs conventionally required before a bin is treated as informative. Nearest-neighbour spacing (1.3-7.7 km) is of the same order as the entire sampled extent (23.6 km), so there is no short-lag information from which to estimate a nugget or a range.

A fitted variogram here would be an artefact of the fitting routine, not a measurement of spatial structure. Worse, the two campaigns form two spatial clusters that differ in landscape type, so any apparent spatial correlation would largely be the peat/mineral contrast reappearing as a distance effect. **No kriged or IDW surface is produced.**

### The Li et al. 2025 trap

The asset is named `McMasterCarbon30mkgm2version1`. Read casually, the `30` invites a direct comparison against SoilGrids OCS **0-30 cm**. It is not a depth: it is the **pixel size**. `04_gee_reference_audit.R` tests that reading by measuring the layer's actual pixel grid, and tests the units by checking value magnitudes against physically plausible ranges for a 0-30 cm stock versus a full peat profile.

A full-profile Hudson Bay Lowlands peat carbon stock is on the order of 50-400 kg C/m2. A 0-30 cm stock in this dataset ranges 2.7-14.3 kg C/m2. These differ by more than an order of magnitude and both look reasonable on a map. The audit refuses to difference, average or validate across layers whose depth support differs, and records the verdict in the extracted data so a downstream reader cannot pick up the column without also picking up the reason it is not comparable.

## What would make this answerable

- **More cores, spread rather than clustered.** Roughly 30-50 cores placed deliberately across the wetness and isostatic-rebound gradients would make a covariate model testable. Eight cores at two accessible sites cannot be rescued by any method.
- **Cores to a common depth, or deeper.** Two of eight stop short of 30 cm. A consistent target depth would remove the lower-bound problem entirely.
- **Full-profile peat cores.** The 0-30 cm window misses nearly all of the carbon in this landscape. Peat depth to the mineral contact would be worth more than any number of shallow cores.
- **One conversion convention, or measured carbon.** Elemental analysis on a subset would calibrate the loss-on-ignition conversion and remove the campaign confound.
- **Cores in the same stratum, on separate campaigns.** This would begin to separate landscape from campaign, which the current design cannot.
